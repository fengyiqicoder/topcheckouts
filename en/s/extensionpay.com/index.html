In 2020 Google announced it would deprecate the built-in payments feature of the Chrome Web Store (shut down in early 2021), "leaving thousands of extension developers who charged through it stranded". Independent developer Glen Chiacchieri (GitHub: Glench), who had built the Chrome extension Dictionary of Numbers and spent time at MIT Media Lab and on Bret Victor's team, registered extensionpay.com in 2020-12 and open-sourced ExtPay.js in 2021-01: add a few lines of JS to an extension, connect your own Stripe, and you can charge one-time, subscription or trial fees with no server of your own, with the platform taking only 5%. The homepage pitched itself directly as a "Chrome Web Store Payments replacement" to catch developers forced to migrate. After that it spread through the open-source repo (784 stars), a recommendation in Mozilla's official extension development docs, and word of mouth through developer blogs and tutorials, plus every paid extension sending its users to a payment page on the extensionpay.com domain (brand exposure, and much of the traffic is referrals from individual extensions). Basics

  • Product: browser extension payments service + open-source JS library ExtPay (MIT): Stripe payments, free trials, one-time payments, subscriptions, login to identify paying users; supports Chrome, Firefox, Edge, Opera and Brave.
  • Founder: Glen Chiacchieri (personal site glench.com: former MIT Media Lab Scratch engineer, researcher on the team that preceded Dynamicland, changed careers to psychotherapist in 2020; built the Chrome extension Dictionary of Numbers). Looks like a one-person project.
  • Pricing: free to sign up, 5% per transaction + Stripe fees (2.9% + 30¢), no monthly fee.
  • Domain: registered 2020-12-19 (RDAP); GitHub repo Glench/ExtPay created 2021-01-08; first product page on Wayback 2021-01-14.
  • Traffic: Taiwan 18.6%, United States 17.4%, Honduras 6%; direct 70%, social 22% (WhatsApp, YouTube, Reddit), search 4.5%; referrals from pokeathlon.com and gameforge.com, mostly extensions/sites that use it for payments sending paying users over.

Where the first 1,000 users (developers) came from: the evidenced sources are the "CWS payments shutdown" event (the site explicitly says developers came because of it) and the open-source GitHub repo. Speculation: the first developers came from discussions after Google's shutdown notice, when people looked for alternatives in the Chromium Extensions discussion group, Reddit r/chrome_extensions and Hacker News, plus the founder's own Twitter (@Glench); the specific launch post was not found.

Growth loop

Evidence:

  • The open-source library is the distribution: developers find it on GitHub and copy a sample extension to integrate.
  • Official endorsement: Mozilla's extension docs recommend it directly.
  • Branded payment page: every extension's paying users open an extensionpay.com payment page (much of the traffic is referrals from extensions/game sites, and the high shares from countries like Taiwan and Honduras are the paying users of a few particular extensions).
  • Developer content: tutorials and retrospectives on dev.to, CSDN and elsewhere spread on their own.

Speculation:

  • Word of mouth among developers ("no backend to write") is the main force; the site itself does almost no SEO (search is only 4.5%).

Revenue and scale clues

  • Cumulative developer revenue (GMV): $13K (2021-11) to $21K (2022-01) to $100K (2023-01) to $300K (2024-01) to $800K+ (2026-10).
  • At a 5% cut, the platform's cumulative revenue is on the order of $40K (speculation); a small, tidy one-person side project.

Confidence and gaps

  • New site written from scratch. The registration/open-source/launch dates and GMV milestones all have hard evidence from RDAP, the GitHub API and year-by-year Wayback snapshots, so credibility is high.
  • Gaps: the launch post (HN/Reddit/Twitter) was not found; the number of integrated extensions is not public; the exact date Google shut down CWS payments is from memory.
