In 2013 Ben Peterson and Ann Pierce in Boulder built a site where strangers anonymously rate your profile photo. At first it covered only professional headshots (LinkedIn), positioned as a "free headshot test tool + research project". The core mechanism is mutual rating: you vote on others' photos to earn credits, and credits buy ratings of your own photos, so the more users there are the faster results come, and anyone who does not want to wait pays for credits. Growth came from "data research as PR": they wrote studies from tens of thousands to hundreds of millions of ratings on the platform (in 2014, 60,000 ratings analyzing 800 professional photos; in 2017, a rebuttal of OKCupid's dating photo study; in 2019, a paper on an AI trained on 100 million ratings), got picked up by Business Insider, The Muse and other media, and asked those who republished to link back. In 2015 it added dating and social categories, catching Tinder and OKCupid users asking "which photo is the most attractive", and Reddit dating subs and looks-obsessed communities (looksmax) have discussed it for years, until the brand name became shorthand for "photo rating".One-line growth story, compiled by us from public sources; speculation is marked separately below.

Basics

  • Product: anonymous human photo ratings in three categories, Business (smart, trustworthy, influential), Social, and Dating (attractive, trustworthy, smart); vote to earn credits for free tests, or buy credits ($9 for 40, $19 for 100, etc.); also a blog, Profiled, and a "Photofeeler University" course.
  • Company: Photofeeler Inc., headquartered in Boulder, Colorado; founders Ben Peterson and Ann Pierce (first CEO). Funding not found.
  • Technology: in 2019 Agastya Kalra and Ben Peterson published the Photofeeler-D3 neural network paper (arXiv, Springer), trained on 1.2 million dating photos, and the blog says it is based on "100 million ratings".
  • Domain: registered 2013-02-25, first Wayback capture 2013-02-28 (default page), a real page appeared in 2013-11.

Growth loop

Evidence:

  • Mutual-rating credit loop: voting on others' photos earns your own ratings, so users are both supply and demand (stated on the official site and Wikipedia).
  • Data research as PR: studies built from the platform's rating data get media coverage, blog posts ask for a link back, and since 2015 the homepage has carried an "as seen in" media wall.
  • Category shorthand: Reddit (r/Tinder and others), the looksmax forum and competitor articles all use it as the benchmark; in our data direct visits are 80.8% and brand keywords are 91.5% of search.
  • Long-tail SEO: non-brand keywords such as "websites where women rate men" and "youtube profile picture size", matching blog content.

Speculation:

  • The first users probably came from LinkedIn job seekers and professional blog readers (it covered only professional headshots at first); after dating was added in 2015, Reddit dating subs became the main word-of-mouth source.
  • The spread of dating apps (Tinder took off after 2012) was an external tailwind.
  • After AI rating tools appeared in 2023, competitors all compare "real people vs AI", which in turn gives it exposure.

Revenue and scale clues

  • About 100 million ratings cumulatively by 2019 (official blog title).
  • Credit prices: $9/40, $19/100, etc.
  • Our data: United States 21.5%, Germany 17.7%, United Kingdom 15%.
  • Revenue and user count not found.

Confidence and gaps

  • The founders, founding date, research coverage and product evolution have first-hand evidence from Wikipedia and Wayback, and are credible.
